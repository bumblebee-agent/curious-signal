Next, from TechCrunch AI: Recent widespread technical outages affecting major U.S. entities, including Apple Pay, NASA, the Federal Aviation Administration, and Microsoft, have disrupted daily operations across critical infrastructure. Most affected companies have attributed the failures to technical issues rather than malicious external attacks. Industry experts suggest the outages stem from the growing pains of integrating modern AI tools with legacy systems that were not designed for such connectivity.

Also, from The Verge: Apple is developing an agentic artificial intelligence assistant capable of performing complex tasks such as web browsing, cross-app operations, coding, and service calls. The company posted a job listing for a senior engineering manager to lead this initiative, as reported by Bloomberg. This move positions Apple to compete with existing AI agents from Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google, which already allow users to control computers through dedicated applications. Apple has previously upgraded Siri to handle on-screen questions and perform actions across apps, and current reports suggest the assistant will soon gain additional capabilities like image rotation and Safari tab organization.

Then, from TechCrunch AI: Google announced on Thursday the availability of computer use capabilities within its Gemini desktop application for macOS, enabling the AI agent to navigate native Mac applications and browse websites in the background. This launch follows recent expansions of agentic features by competitors including OpenAI, Anthropic, and Meta, as well as Google’s own broader rollout of Gemini Tasks earlier this week. The company also recently began admitting early testers to its revamped CC AI agent, a tool designed to help families manage household organization across email, calendars, and tasks. Computer use allows the AI to execute multistep actions involving local files, apps, and web resources while displaying a live preview of its activity within the user's chat interface.

Worth noting, from Hacker News: Robert Peake, a software engineer and writer, argues that artificial intelligence is triggering a crisis of faith in the software engineering profession. He compares the current shift in coding to the historical transition from hand tools to power machinery in carpentry, noting that automation has scaled production while devaluing craftsmanship. Peake challenges the assumption that AI primarily threatens manual or unskilled labor, asserting instead that the technology impacts intellectual work with significant force. He demonstrates this point by describing how he cloned his own voice for a small cost, achieving a result that his musician wife could not distinguish from his natural speech.

Another item, from MIT News AI: An international team led by researchers at MIT and the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute examined nearly 4,000 clinical prediction studies to evaluate how researchers share the data and code underlying their work. The review, published in Nature Medicine, identified significant gaps in transparency, revealing that code sharing remains uncommon and that publicly available code often lacks the documentation necessary for others to understand and reuse it. The researchers utilized large language models to screen papers for statements about code availability and assessed the corresponding repositories for completeness and clarity. Thomas Sounack, the study’s first author, noted that authors discuss and link to code in highly inconsistent ways, making automated extraction and verification essential for analyzing such a large volume of unstructured information.

Also in the mix, from OpenAI Blog: A technology company has introduced the Performance Factory, an autonomous system designed to maintain software performance at the speed of agentic development. The system utilizes Codex and internal models to automatically detect, investigate, and resolve performance regressions. This architecture allows engineering teams to manage a growing roadmap without being hindered by the unsustainable pace of manual performance engineering. The development of this system followed a progression from manual labor to full automation.

Finally, from Latent Space: Google DeepMind released Gemini 4 Neon, the fourth model in its Gemini 4 series, which scored 61 on the Intelligence Index. The company retired the Argon model and discontinued the older Pro and Flash versions from the Gemini 1-3 lineage. Neon operates as a mid-tier option that is faster and cheaper than the Carbon model.
