AI's expanding influence spans from high-stakes mathematical proofs and fusion energy investments to major legal settlements over social media addiction.

First, from Hacker News: Authors L. Alpöge and T. Buckmaster published a 79-page paper titled Finite-time blow-up for the Navier–Stokes equations on November 20, 2026. The repository hosts this paper alongside a Lean 4 formalization package that utilizes Mathlib version 4.33.1. A README file within the Lean directory details how the formal statements correspond to specific sections of the paper and highlights differences between the formal and written proofs. The formalization includes a large library of approximately 2,270 files and 600,000 lines, and building it requires significant computational resources and time. Statement files define theorems with placeholder proofs, while a solution file derives these results from the Breakdown library.

Next, from Hacker News: Brian Conrad shared a proposed proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis by Alessandro Masullo at the American Mathematical Society Executive Committee and Board of Trustees meeting on November 20, 2026. Conrad has spent months reviewing Masullo’s work and provided extensive feedback that Masullo is currently incorporating into the draft. The author completed the proof manually without using artificial intelligence. Conrad expressed strong confidence in the validity of the work based on his thorough examination of the material.

Then, from The Verge: YouTube is preparing to pay $3 billion to thousands of school districts to settle claims that its platform design contributed to social media addiction among young users. Bloomberg reports that the company reached the agreement in principle, which also mandates the implementation of new safeguards for teenagers. This financial resolution allows Google to avoid litigation from US school districts that accuse YouTube, along with Meta, Snap, and TikTok, of negatively impacting teen mental health. The next scheduled trial is set for February 2027 in Oakland, California, and will feature Meta, Snap, and TikTok as defendants.

Worth noting, from TechCrunch AI: Spotify co-founder Daniel Ek has invested in Europa Fusion, a United Kingdom-based fusion power startup that operates in stealth mode. The company was established in February by Nantas Nardelli, an artificial intelligence researcher who previously held leadership roles at Google DeepMind, Facebook AI Research, and Microsoft Research Cambridge. Nardelli most recently served as the head of machine learning at Pacific Fusion, a Fremont, California, firm that has recently begun construction on a demonstration facility in New Mexico. Nardelli departed Pacific Fusion because he disagreed with the industry standard of relying heavily on physical hardware testing to refine simulations. He believes fusion developers should instead prioritize a simulation- and data-heavy methodology.

Another item, from Hugging Face Blog: Thinking Machines Lab has released Inkling 2 Mini, a multimodal Mixture-of-Experts model designed for local AI setups. The model features approximately 26 billion physical total parameters and activates only 3 billion parameters per token. It supports a context size of up to 1 million tokens and accepts both image and text inputs. The architecture utilizes a looping mechanism where the 12 middle layers of its 24 decoder layers execute three times per token. This design allows the model to behave as if it possesses 48 logical layers while maintaining only 24 physical ones. Each pass through the looped core reuses the same weights and refines information via cross-pass messages.

Also in the mix, from MIT News AI: Jim Glass, a senior research scientist in the MIT Computer Science and Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, co-led an MIT-IBM Computing Research Lab investigation into the performance gaps of state-of-the-art multimodal artificial intelligence models. The research team focused on overcoming these limitations through data-efficient methods and novel benchmarks rather than simply scaling up model sizes. Glass explained that the project aimed to explore the relationship between perception and language without requiring large amounts of annotated training data. The team developed techniques to help models re-emerge latent capabilities that might disappear during fine-tuning processes.

Shifting gears, from Latent Space: The AI industry’s 2026 word of the year is “slop,” defined as low-quality output from large language models. Software engineers now face daily concerns about avoiding such output as AI-generated code becomes commonplace. Formal verification systems offer a solution by using mathematical proofs to verify software correctness. Several AI companies now offer these systems, including Harmonic, Theorem, Axiom Math, and the new entrant &Truth. Clark Barrett, the co-founder and co-CEO of &Truth, serves as a leading proponent of this technology. He holds the title of Corbel Professor of Computer Science at Stanford and co-created the automated reasoning engine cvc5.

Finally, from OpenAI Blog: OpenAI has strengthened its defenses against adversarial distillation, a technique that allows bad actors to reproduce the capabilities of frontier AI models without inheriting their safety safeguards. Adversarial distillation enables an adversary to train a new model on the outputs or protected reasoning of an existing model, potentially creating a tool that can perform dangerous tasks, such as cyberattacks, that the original model would have refused. To combat this threat, OpenAI has updated the safeguards in its GPT-6.2 Astra and GPT-6.2 Sol models, focusing heavily on preventing the extraction of protected reasoning traces, which are the intermediate steps a model generates internally to reach an answer. The company reports that these updated safeguards significantly reduced the average attack success rate for recovering protected reasoning from 54.0 percent in the previous GPT-6 Astra model to 0.2 percent in GPT-6.2 Astra.
