### On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem

OpenAI announced that an internal AI system produced a proof resolving the Navier-Stokes existence and smoothness problem, one of the seven Millennium Prize Problems. The system demonstrated that the dynamics of three-dimensional incompressible fluid motion can develop a singularity in finite time, meaning fluid speeds grow without bound despite the presence of viscosity. This finding addresses a question that has remained unresolved for approximately 90 years and establishes statement C and D in the official Millennium Prize formulation. The proof identifies a vortex structure that spirals inward and elongates, causing the central region to shrink and speed up while maintaining finite energy. OpenAI released both a written writeup of the analytical proof and a formalization in the Lean proof assistant to verify the results. The company stated that the proof was generated by an internal model significantly more capable than GPT-6 Astra, which was undergoing training at the time. The effort involved a multi-agent system with approximately 10,000 concurrent agents that communicated and collaborated to solve the problem. These agents sent 2.7 million messages and used about 130 billion output tokens during the resolution process, which took roughly 88 hours. Prior to solving the Navier-Stokes problem, the same agent system resolved the regularity problem for the unforced Euler equations, a related question involving fluid motion without viscosity. OpenAI initiated the evaluation after hearing rumors that other researchers had also made progress on Millennium Prize problems. The company reached out to Levent Alpöge, an Anthropic employee, and Tristan Buckmaster, a mathematics professor at NYU, to coordinate a joint announcement. OpenAI acknowledged the priority of Alpöge and Buckmaster’s work on the forced Euler problem, noting that their results differed significantly from OpenAI’s findings. The company emphasized that it did not access any specific user data from the other researchers and that the proofs were developed independently. OpenAI stated that the release aims to inform the public about the pace of AI progress rather than to claim the Millennium Prize. The organization described the result as a snapshot of current capabilities and reaffirmed its commitment to building AI systems that are steerable, accountable, and beneficial to humanity. ([On the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem](https://openai.com/index/navier-stokes-solution/)).

### Meta bets on AI agent Muse to catch up in AI race

Meta has launched Muse, a personal artificial intelligence assistant designed to handle everyday tasks such as online shopping, email management, and trip planning. The company positions Muse as a strategic move to revitalize its standing in the AI market against competitors like OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google. Users can interact with the assistant through the Muse app or WhatsApp, and the tool operates autonomously to complete goals, including filling out forms and negotiating on behalf of the user. Meta states that Muse will continue working in the background for lengthy tasks and will return to the user if approval is needed for actions like purchases. The service is rolling out in the United States on iOS and Android devices, with support for muse.ai and upcoming AI glasses. Meta plans to offer Muse for free to most users, while introducing paid subscriptions for those who require additional capabilities, though specific pricing and limits remain undefined. The assistant runs on Meta’s in-house Muse Spark model and utilizes a virtual computer in the cloud to isolate user data. Meta claims that Muse does not have visibility into passwords or payment methods and employs a secondary agent named Sentinel to monitor activity. Users can opt out of having their interactions used to train Meta’s AI models and can instruct the assistant to forget specific information. The company intends to release a more secure, encrypted version of the virtual machine later this year. Despite these features, Meta faces significant trust challenges due to past privacy scandals and previous AI missteps, including a support chatbot exploit and controversial content feeds. ### Safety for Whom? Refusing the Right Subset of a Topic, Not the Whole Topic

A recent paper from Multiverse Computing challenges the industry standard of treating safety alignment as a broad topic-level classification. The research argues that guard models like LlamaGuard-3 fail to distinguish between harmful and benign requests within the same subject, such as politics. This limitation forces deployments to either refuse all political prompts or allow dangerous manipulation, leaving no middle ground for nuanced policy enforcement. The study introduces a method called boundary-aware self-distillation to train models on specific subsets of harmful content rather than entire topics. The authors identify three critical flaws in standard self-generated safety tuning pipelines. First, single-shot generation often fails to produce refusals for difficult prompts, leading to a significant coverage gap where hard examples are silently discarded. The researchers implemented an escalating retry strategy to recover these lost prompts, reducing failure rates from nearly 20 percent to 0.20 percent. Second, safety tuning frequently causes false refusals on benign prompts that contain dangerous-looking language. To counter this, the team incorporated in-distribution benign data into the training set to help the model distinguish intent from surface-level wording. Third, traditional metrics do not measure the shape of the decision boundary between safe and unsafe content. The study utilizes held-out pairs of harmful and benign prompts to evaluate how well a model separates these categories. Experiments on the Qwen3-8B model demonstrate that improving harmful refusal rates can drastically increase over-refusal on safe prompts. Without careful data composition, the model refused nearly three-quarters of plainly safe prompts in the XSTest benchmark. The research shows that replacing externally adopted compliance responses with verified responses generated by the target model itself significantly reduces this over-refusal. Additionally, using specific benign boundary data during training lowered false refusals on compliant prompts from 32.94 percent to 4.16 percent while maintaining high refusal rates on harmful content. The authors conclude that safety tuning must be assessed by measuring both sides of the boundary to ensure models remain useful for legitimate requests. ([Safety for Whom? Refusing the Right Subset of a Topic, Not the Whole Topic](https://huggingface.co/blog/MultiverseComputingCAI/safety-for-whom)).

- [How GPT-5.6 Sol helps run quantum computing experiments](https://openai.com/index/codex-quantum-computing-experiments): Beatriz Yankelevich, a graduate student in MIT’s Engineering Quantum Systems Group, utilized the GPT-5.6 Sol model integrated with Codex to automate the calibration of superconducting qubits. The research team at MIT studies these qubits, which operate near absolute zero inside dilution refrigerators and require precise control via microwave signals. Yankelevich connected the AI system to the laboratory software to enable autonomous measurement, data analysis, and experimental decision-making. The AI agent successfully completed standard calibration sequences on an uncalibrated six-qubit chip by identifying transition frequencies and determining quantum information retention times. This automation allowed the system to run routine measurements overnight or during other laboratory tasks with minimal researcher intervention. The agent performed effectively when experimental signals were clear, but it struggled with weak or noisy data that required human guidance to interpret. The project demonstrates that AI agents can handle well-defined experimental workflows, thereby freeing researchers to focus on higher-level tasks such as result interpretation and experiment design. Yankelevich noted that the infrastructure she built allows multiple agents to work on different problems simultaneously while she manages broader research goals.

De Swardt observed that his usage increased significantly on days when he performed no work and had disabled all connected services. He contacted Anthropic to request an itemized list of his usage, but the company did not provide one. Instead, Anthropic suspended his account, invalidated his tokens, and issued a partial refund of £44.49. The suspension disrupted De Swardt’s business operations, which rely heavily on AI agents for administrative and coding tasks. Anthropic later informed De Swardt that a compromised session key had been used to mint unauthorized tokens for a third-party service. The company stated that the evidence suggested either stolen credentials or a connection to an outside service, though it could not determine how access was obtained. De Swardt posted his experience on Reddit, where other users reported similar incidents of sudden, unexplained token depletion. Some users received emails from Anthropic warning them about infostealer malware that steals login sessions. Anthropic explained that such malware can be acquired from various online sources unrelated to the Claude platform. The company typically responds to such incidents by signing users out, invalidating authorizations, and issuing refunds. De Swardt did not receive such a warning and maintains that his computer was not compromised. He reinstated his account after two weeks but ultimately cancelled his subscription in favor of Cursor. De Swardt criticized Anthropic for lacking tools that allow users to monitor token consumption in detail. He stated that the company declined to comment on how users can identify misuse.

- [Kimi K3 (2.8T) at 1 token/s on a MacBook Pro, streamed from four SSDs](https://github.com/argonautlabsai/deltafin): Deltafin is an open-source engine that runs the full 2.8-trillion-parameter Kimi K3 model on consumer hardware without pruning or quantizing the weights. The project demonstrates that the model can operate on a MacBook Pro equipped with an M5 Max chip and 128 GB of memory by streaming expert weights from solid-state drives. Benchmarks conducted in September 2026 show a steady decode speed of approximately one token per second for a 512-token response. The system requires roughly six minutes to generate the first token of a long prompt due to inefficient prefill operations that re-read layer experts multiple times. Performance scales with the number of storage drives, with four drives providing the baseline speed and a single drive delivering about half that throughput. The engine uses a small draft model called DSpark to guess output tokens, which the main K3 model then verifies to maintain exact quality. An optional Qwen add-on further accelerates raw text completion by allowing the smaller model to propose tokens for K3 to check. The software includes a native server that implements a subset of the OpenAI API for local integration. Developers can build the tool from source using Rust and configure it to stream model weights on demand to save disk space. The project emphasizes that speed improvements must never compromise the fidelity of the original model released by Moonshot AI.
