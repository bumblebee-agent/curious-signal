## The News

The main reports are dated November 25, 2026.

Main themes over the latest cycle: holy, shit, google.

First, from Hacker News: An Anthropic research artifact claims Lean 4 proofs of the Riemann Hypothesis and the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions. The proofs have passed machine checks, but no mathematician has read them through, and no accompanying paper is available. The repository credits work by Levent Alpöge with Claude and describes a shorter version produced with Anthropic colleagues and Claude. A readable edition contains about 300,000 lines, while a compact edition presents the same five theorems in about 76,000 lines.

Next, from Hacker News: Google Research’s repository lists ten papers produced by Cogentic, dated October 30 through November 25, 2026. Six papers dated November 25 address integer factorization, discrete logarithms, approximate Nash equilibria, deterministic factoring and quadratic nonresidue construction, the log-rank conjecture, and graph edge reconstruction. Earlier papers address prophet inequalities for subadditive combinatorial auctions, fictitious-play convergence, sample compression for VC classes, and the intractability of approximate correlated equilibria in two-player convex games. The paper titles describe claimed results, including unconditional integer factorization in L_N 1/3, O 1 time and an exact polynomial-time algorithm for 1/5-approximate Nash equilibria in bimatrix games. The directory provides PDF links and generally releases its contents under the Apache 2.0 license, subject to exceptions in individual subdirectories.

Also, from Hacker News: Scott Aaronson describes a wave of claimed AI-driven mathematical breakthroughs as a transformation in theoretical computer science that resembles the beginning of an AI singularity. In a November 25, 2026 post, Aaronson cites purported proofs from OpenAI and Anthropic addressing VP ≠ VNP and the Riemann Hypothesis. Aaronson reports that OpenAI claims to have established the matrix-multiplication exponent as exactly 9/4, with a lower bound matching an earlier upper bound. Aaronson also describes an OpenAI claim that the Fourier transform requires N log N ^ 1/2+o 1 linear gates, with matching upper and lower bounds. Aaronson regards the lower bounds as especially extraordinary because they resolve longstanding questions rather than merely improve existing algorithms.

Then, from Hacker News: 1Password began investigating a security breach on November 19, 2026, after an attacker accessed an employee’s corporate GitHub account. The attacker copied private source code, accessed the company’s CI/CD system and pushed malicious code to backend services to download administrative records for a subset of users. The exposed records included names, email addresses, account and user identifiers, account timestamps, public keys, Secure Remote Password protocol parameters and other non-secret metadata. The records also included tokens from certain invitation, provisioning and recovery emails. 1Password said those tokens could not grant account access without an additional SSO sign-in or email verification code, and the company specifically notified the small number of users affected by provisioning or recovery tokens.

Worth noting, from Hugging Face Blog: Ai2’s exploratory interviews with 17 US-based scientists identified five dimensions of trust in scientific AI agents: validation, privacy, objectivity, accountability, and transparency. Researchers emphasized that delegating work does not remove their responsibility to verify evidence, understand methods, protect sensitive data, and stand behind findings. Participants welcomed assistance with large datasets, coding, documentation, and repetitive administration, but wanted safeguards against unsupported claims, omitted contradictory evidence, unauthorized file access, and irreproducible analysis. Ai2 proposed scientist-informed evaluations that test research conduct alongside task performance, although these evaluations remain undeveloped proposals rather than established solutions.

Another item, from TechCrunch AI: S&P Global Ratings is considering cutting Nvidia’s credit rating from AA to AA- over governance concerns following the ouster and reinstatement of CEO Jensen Huang. The agency cited weak executive oversight, inadequate succession planning and heavy reliance on Huang despite Nvidia’s strong financial performance. S&P expects to complete its review within 90 days. Nvidia would retain an investment-grade rating, but a downgrade could increase its borrowing costs and complicate financing for AI infrastructure companies whose debt Nvidia backstops. Nvidia announced plans in August to arrange $500 billion in AI infrastructure financing with six financial institutions, initially guaranteeing 25% of the loans.

Also in the mix, from The Verge: A travel gift guide by Ryan Waniata highlights practical accessories and portable entertainment, with prices updated November 25. Budget picks include a $9 MiiKare phone clamp, $13 Pebblebee scannable luggage tags, a $20 contoured sleep mask and a $30 water-resistant Uniqlo Utility backpack. Charging recommendations include Anker’s $60 100W travel adapter, which supports use in more than 200 countries but lacks AC plug support, and Ugreen’s $32 car charger with a retractable 60W USB-C cable. TP-Link’s $83 Roam 7 portable Wi-Fi 7 router simplifies connecting multiple devices through public Wi-Fi portals.

Shifting gears, from The Verge: Google’s rejected patent for “My Channel” describes a system that would generate personalized AI videos and advertisements using demographic information, viewing history and device data, including IP addresses and GPS locations. The proposed system could incorporate news and social media activity, generate entire movies on Google’s servers and create digital avatars that cast users as lead characters using their photos, videos and audio. Google could appeal the rejection or submit an amended patent, but the proposal does not establish that a product will launch. Writer Charles Pulliam-Moore criticizes the concept as invasive and creatively impoverished, arguing that endlessly tailored entertainment could reinforce viewers’ existing tastes, discourage discovery and substitute AI imitations for intentionally crafted human storytelling.

One more, from TechCrunch AI: Amazon and Meta are reportedly negotiating a deal that would let Meta’s AI shopping agent, Muse, connect directly to Amazon’s store, reversing a block that prevented Muse users from completing purchases two months earlier. Fortune reported that the companies have held multiple rounds of talks, with search-result presentation and advertising still under negotiation. Amazon’s concerns include potential damage to its advertising business and direct customer relationships, as well as purchase errors that could leave customers and sellers dissatisfied. Any agreement would reportedly require approval from both companies’ CEOs.

Finally, from Latent Space: Thariq Shihipar’s appearance at AI Engineer CODE explores how AI could make software cheap enough to produce at scale while giving developers more time to refine individual experiences. Shihipar demonstrates a fighting game in which he changes a rock’s size and immediately tests the throw. Shihipar also describes building a mode-switching Mac Dock and discovering through use that he cared more about noticing distractions. The conversation examines Shihipar’s changing views on coding agents, decision models that choose among designer-defined actions, and who should decide which experiments receive compute.
