### Launch HN: Onlook (YC W25) – An open-source developer tool for designers

Onlook is an open-source desktop application that allows users to design websites and prototypes using artificial intelligence on a canvas built for creative work. The tool functions as a local-first alternative to platforms like Figma and Webflow by running directly on a user’s computer without requiring cloud keys or external accounts. It operates by opening any Git repository on the machine and starting the developer’s own dev server to display the live application. Designers can visually shape the interface and explore ideas side by side using mobile, tablet, and desktop frames. The platform lets users connect their own AI agents, such as Claude Code or ChatGPT, to generate code and edit real files in the repository. Users can describe desired changes in a chat interface, and the connected agent arranges variations and states on the canvas. All modifications are tracked by Git, ensuring that the designs correspond to the actual product codebase rather than separate mockups. The application supports exporting artifacts as self-contained HTML, PNG, JPEG, or PDF files for review or sharing. Onlook runs on macOS for both Apple Silicon and Intel x64 systems, with Windows and Linux versions currently available via waitlists. The project is released under the Apache 2.0 license, allowing users to fork and customize the source code for their own use. ### These tech outages are giving Y2K, AI agent style

Recent widespread technical outages affecting major U.S. entities, including Apple Pay, NASA, the Federal Aviation Administration, and Microsoft, have disrupted daily operations across critical infrastructure. These disruptions followed a period of heightened cybersecurity concerns known as Black October and initially raised fears of coordinated AI-driven cyberattacks. However, officials including the director of National Intelligence and President Trump have characterized the events as separate incidents rather than state-sponsored terrorism or AI hacker invasions. Most affected companies have attributed the failures to technical issues rather than malicious external attacks. Industry experts suggest the outages stem from the growing pains of integrating modern AI tools with legacy systems that were not designed for such connectivity. Dave DeWalt, a former cybersecurity executive, stated that AI is exposing existing vulnerabilities at an unprecedented speed, prompting rapid patching efforts that temporarily destabilize networks. Anusha Iyer, founder of the security startup Corsha, explained that the FAA outage likely resulted from legacy hardware being overwhelmed during modernization efforts rather than from AI itself. Senator Mark Warner noted that the frequency of these disruptions highlights the challenge of managing the consequences of rapidly advancing AI technology. Experts argue that the root cause is the rushed integration of legacy and modern systems without adequate risk management or segmentation. DeWalt suggested that this painful transition creates opportunities for new companies focused on AI-driven vulnerability management and system resilience. The events may ultimately benefit national security by forcing enterprises to identify and fix flaws before bad actors can exploit them. He compares the current shift in coding to the historical transition from hand tools to power machinery in carpentry, noting that automation has scaled production while devaluing craftsmanship. Peake challenges the assumption that AI primarily threatens manual or unskilled labor, asserting instead that the technology impacts intellectual work with significant force. He demonstrates this point by describing how he cloned his own voice for a small cost, achieving a result that his musician wife could not distinguish from his natural speech. The author identifies a counterintuitive pattern in AI capabilities known as the jagged frontier, where machines excel at tasks that humans find difficult and struggle with tasks humans find easy. He explains this phenomenon through verifier’s law, which states that tasks are easier to automate if they are easy to check or grade. Peake categorizes work into four quadrants based on the ease of grading and the objective agreement on its value. Software code and speech synthesis fall into the category of high grading and high value, making them prime targets for automation. In contrast, poetry and parenting occupy positions where they are either highly valued but ungradeable, or neither easily graded nor objectively valued. Peake notes that market incentives drive the automation of commercial art and writing because these fields rely on agreed-upon deliverables rather than subjective artistic merit. He observes that many illustrators and translators are already losing work to generative AI tools. The author distinguishes between deliverables, which AI can produce, and outcomes, which require complex human social interaction and accountability. He argues that human workers must pivot toward achieving outcomes and maintaining accountability for results. The writer advocates for reskilling strategies that focus on verifying AI-generated work and defining clear objectives before requesting deliverables. - [AI helps researchers investigate how thousands of medical studies share code](https://news.mit.edu/2026/ai-helps-researchers-investigate-how-medical-studies-share-code-1119): An international team led by researchers at MIT and the Dana-Farber Cancer Institute examined nearly 4,000 clinical prediction studies to evaluate how researchers share the data and code underlying their work. The review, published in Nature Medicine, identified significant gaps in transparency, revealing that code sharing remains uncommon and that publicly available code often lacks the documentation necessary for others to understand and reuse it. The researchers utilized large language models to screen papers for statements about code availability and assessed the corresponding repositories for completeness and clarity. Thomas Sounack, the study’s first author, noted that authors discuss and link to code in highly inconsistent ways, making automated extraction and verification essential for analyzing such a large volume of unstructured information. MIT research scientist Tom Pollard, a senior author of the study, emphasized that the large language models enabled the team to process scattered information across papers and repositories with greater detail than previous methods allowed. The analysis found that only about one in eight studies reported sharing code, and where code was shared, the quality varied from extremely well-documented to offering almost no guidance. Journal practices differed substantially, with code sharing rates ranging from zero to more than 70 percent, although some journals with low sharing rates still had written policies, suggesting that guidelines alone do not guarantee compliance. To address these inconsistencies, the findings are informing the development of TRIPOD-Code, a new reporting guideline designed to establish a consensus on essential elements for sharing analytical code. The upcoming guidelines aim to provide practical guidance for researchers and clearer expectations for journals to improve reproducibility in clinical prediction models.
